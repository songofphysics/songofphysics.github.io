The Autodidact's Bookshelf

A library of book recommendations for self study in mathematical physics.

Teaching ourselves mathematics is a strangely lonely business. Nobody tells us which book to open next, and the internet is not much help either, since half of it recommends the same three titles to everyone and the other half recommends whatever it happened to suffer through in college. This post is my attempt at something better. It is a shelf of books, lecture notes and courses that we can work through, more or less in order, to get from school mathematics to the mathematics that modern theoretical physics is actually written in.

The shelf is split into phases, and each phase into subjects. The books within a subject are companions rather than a checklist, so pick one as the main text and keep another nearby for the days when the first one stops making sense (there will be such days). The phases are a rough guide to prerequisites and not a syllabus. Reading Phase 2 algebra alongside Phase 3 analysis is perfectly fine, and frankly it is how most of us end up doing it anyway.

Wherever something is legally free online, I have linked it. Every entry also has a spot for a proper review, by which I mean one written by somebody who has actually read the book and not a row of stars on a shopping site. Those links will trickle in over time. And yes, I know, I know, the list is long. Sigh. So is mathematics.

Phase 0 (Pre Uni Bridge)

Most of us were trained at school to compute. See the question, recall the method, write the answer and move on to the next one before the bell rings. University mathematics wants something else entirely, namely proofs, and the jump can feel brutal when nobody warns us about it. Consider this phase the warning. It assumes nothing beyond school algebra and calculus, so strong high school students and anyone returning to mathematics after a long break should start here. We learn how to read and write a proof, meet discrete mathematics properly, take a second and far more honest look at precalculus and calculus, and keep a serious physics text open so that the mathematics always has something to talk about.

High School & Bridge

Book of Proof, Richard Hammack. 3rd ed., self-published, 2018.

Hammack’s book is simply the best free introduction to mathematical logic and proofs. It also helps that it assumes we have already seen some calculus, as many high schoolers have, so its examples can lean on familiar ground. He spends just enough time on sets, logic and counting to get going, then gives each proof technique (direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, induction) its own chapter with dozens of worked examples, before putting them to use on relations, functions and cardinality. The later chapter on proofs in calculus is a gentle first taste of the epsilons and deltas waiting for us in Phase 1. Odd-numbered exercises have solutions at the back, so it works well for self-study.

Review to follow. · Free online

Applied Discrete Structures, Al Doerr, Ken Levasseur. Version 3.14, self-published (Lulu), 2026.

This one started life as a textbook for computer science students, and it shows in the best way. Alongside the usual logic, combinatorics, relations, graphs and recursion, it pushes on into algebraic structures (groups, monoids, Boolean algebras, rings) and automata, which most discrete mathematics books never reach. The free online version comes with live Sage cells, so we can run and modify the computations right on the page. It is a good choice if we want discrete mathematics as a doorway into algebra rather than as a collection of counting tricks, and it pairs nicely with Hammack, who supplies the proof-writing habits it takes for granted.

Review to follow. · Free online

Precalculus, Carl Stitz, Jeff Zeager. Edition 4i, self-published.

Why bother with precalculus after school? Because school usually teaches it as a list of formulae, and Stitz and Zeager teach it as the study of functions. Every family of functions (polynomial, rational, exponential, logarithmic, trigonometric) is examined for its domain, its behaviour and its graph, with worked examples that show every step and a mountain of exercises with answers. The book is long and wordy, so it is better used to fill specific gaps than read cover to cover. The newer 4i edition adds some introductory calculus. Anyone who feels shaky about trigonometric identities or logarithms should spend a few weeks here before touching analysis.

Review to follow. · Free online

Differential and Integral Calculus, Vol. 1, N. Piskunov. 2nd ed. (transl. Yankovsky), 2 vols, Mir (Indian reprint: Aargon Press 2018), 1974.

Piskunov wrote for Soviet engineering colleges, and the book has been a fixture of Indian engineering libraries for decades thanks to Mir and its reprints. It is not a rigorous analysis text and does not pretend to be one. What it offers is calculus as a working tool, with clear geometric and physical motivation and an enormous number of fully worked problems. The first volume covers limits, differentiation, the study of functions and integration with its applications. Use it alongside Hammack. Piskunov teaches us to compute fluently, Hammack teaches us to prove, and Phase 1 needs both.

Review to follow. · Free online

Physics, Vol. 1, David Halliday, Robert Resnick, Kenneth S. Krane. 5th ed., Wiley, 2002.

Not to be confused with the ‘Fundamentals’ edition that every coaching institute hands out. This is the original, more demanding Halliday–Resnick line, revised by Krane, and it uses calculus freely and without apology. Derivations are given in full, concepts are discussed before formulae appear, and the problems range from routine to genuinely hard. The first volume covers mechanics, waves, fluids and thermodynamics. Reading it next to Piskunov makes a strong case for learning calculus properly, because we watch every idea in one book being used immediately in the other.

Review to follow.

Differential and Integral Calculus, Vol. 2, N. Piskunov. 2nd ed. (transl. Yankovsky), 2 vols, Mir (Indian reprint: Aargon Press 2018), 1974.

The second volume is where Piskunov becomes indispensable for a budding physicist. It covers ordinary differential equations, multiple, line and surface integrals, series including Fourier series, the classic equations of mathematical physics, and even short chapters on operational calculus, probability and matrices. Again the emphasis is on method and worked examples rather than proof. When a physics text casually says ‘integrating over the surface’ and we have no idea how, this is the book to open.

Review to follow. · Free online

Physics, Vol. 2, David Halliday, Robert Resnick, Kenneth S. Krane. 5th ed., Wiley, 2002.

The second volume covers electricity, magnetism, optics and the beginnings of modern physics. Electromagnetism is where school physics usually collapses into memorised formulae, and Krane’s careful use of vector calculus is the antidote. Students preparing for olympiads will find that the problems here sit comfortably between the textbook exercises and Irodov.

Review to follow.

Phase 1 (Basics)

This is roughly the first year of a mathematics degree, minus the courses that seem to exist mainly to fill a timetable. Sets, rigorous real analysis, linear algebra done properly, probability and a little programming make up the foundation that everything else rests on. If Phase 0 taught us what a proof is, Phase 1 is where we write a few hundred of them.

Foundations 1 (Sets)

Classic Set Theory: For Guided Independent Study, Derek Goldrei. 1st ed., Chapman & Hall/CRC (Routledge reprint 2017), 1996.

Goldrei wrote this for the Open University, which means it was designed from the start for people studying alone. The structure shows it. Each idea is introduced with a motivating question, developed in short steps, and immediately tested with exercises, many of which come with solutions. The book constructs the real numbers from the rationals and the natural numbers from sets, explains why the paradoxes forced mathematicians to axiomatise, and then works through the ZF axioms, the axiom of choice, ordinals and cardinals. It is gentler than most set theory books and far more honest than the half-page ‘sets and relations’ chapter we met at school.

Review to follow.

Analysis 1 (Real)

Basic Analysis I: Introduction to Real Analysis, Volume I, Jiří Lebl. Version 6.3, self-published, 2026.

Lebl wrote this for his own course at Oklahoma State, keeps it free online and revises it as errata come in. It stays on the real line for as long as possible, through sequences and series, continuity, the derivative and the Riemann integral (done the Darboux way), and saves metric spaces for the final chapter. His reasoning is that abstraction sinks in better once the concrete case feels familiar. There are over 500 exercises, with WeBWorK sets available online, but no answers, so it works best with a course or a study group, or with Cummings and Abbott nearby for a second explanation. It assumes a proof course, so Hammack comes first.

Review to follow. · Free online

Real Analysis: A Long-Form Mathematics Textbook, Jay Cummings. 2nd ed. (2 + ε, 2024 update), Independently published, 2019.

Cummings writes the way a good teacher talks at the board, which is exactly what most analysis books refuse to do. Ideas are motivated, examples are discussed at length, and proofs are often preceded by the rough ‘scratch work’ that shows how one actually finds a proof before writing it up neatly. That last habit alone makes the book valuable, because nobody else shows it. It covers the standard first course (the real numbers, sequences, series, continuity, differentiation, integration, sequences of functions). Read it first if analysis feels intimidating, then move to Abbott for a tighter treatment.

Review to follow.

Understanding Analysis, Stephen Abbott. 2nd ed., Springer (UTM), 2015.

Abbott’s great trick is to open every chapter with a discussion of why we should care, usually a puzzle or a paradox about infinite sums or strange functions, before any definition appears. The proofs that follow are clean and the exercises are excellent. The book is deliberately limited to one variable and to a first course, but the closing chapter and the end-of-chapter projects (on topics such as Euler’s sum, the Weierstrass approximation theorem and the gamma function) give a taste of what lies beyond. It makes a lovely, lighter companion to Zorich.

Review to follow.

Mathematical Analysis I, Vladimir A. Zorich. 2nd ed., Springer (Universitext), 2015.

If I had to recommend a single analysis book, it would be this one, for its comprehensiveness and its constant attention to applications (I am a physicist by trade, after all). Zorich’s course comes from Moscow State University, and it is the most complete analysis text on this shelf. Where most books stop at proofs, Zorich keeps going with remarks, physical applications, historical comments and problems that link analysis to mechanics and the natural sciences. The first volume covers the real numbers, limits, continuity, differential calculus in one variable, integration and the start of calculus in several variables. Its MAA reviewer called it overkill. I know, I know, it is a lot. But a book that keeps showing us why the theorems matter outside mathematics is worth every extra page, and Cummings and Abbott are right there whenever we need a gentler second explanation.

Review to follow.

Algebra 1 (Linear)

Linear Algebra Done Right, Sheldon Axler. 4th ed., Springer (UTM), 2024.

Axler’s book is built on a single provocative idea, that determinants obscure more than they explain. So eigenvalues are proved to exist without them, operators take centre stage instead of matrices, and determinants appear only at the end, defined in a way that makes their properties obvious. The payoff is a beautiful treatment of inner product spaces and the spectral theorem, which is exactly the linear algebra that quantum mechanics and functional analysis need. The latest edition adds multilinear algebra and is open access, with Axler’s own videos online. The one drawback is that it is light on computation, so pair it with Treil or Shilov for practice with actual matrices.

Review to follow. · Free online

Linear Algebra Done Wrong, Sergei Treil. 2017 revision, self-published, 2017.

The title is a joke at Axler’s expense, and the book is a useful counterweight to him. Treil, who wrote it for an honours course at Brown, keeps determinants in their traditional place and is far more willing to work with matrices, while still being rigorous. He also covers material that many first courses skip, such as bilinear and quadratic forms and the structure of operators in inner product spaces. Read the two side by side and we get both the elegant operator viewpoint and the practical matrix one.

Review to follow. · Free online

Linear Algebra, Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel, Lawrence E. Spence. 5th ed., Pearson, 2019.

Friedberg, Insel and Spence is the standard proof-based linear algebra text in many universities, and for good reason. It is complete, careful and full of exercises, going all the way to the Jordan and rational canonical forms, with short application sections (Markov chains, least squares, differential equations) along the way. The style is dry and the book can feel like a reference rather than a story. Use it as the main text if we want everything in one place, or as the problem bank to go alongside Axler.

Review to follow.

Linear Algebra, Georgi E. Shilov. Dover reprint, Dover, 1977.

Shilov represents the classical Russian route through linear algebra, starting with determinants and systems of linear equations and climbing steadily to linear spaces, quadratic forms, Euclidean and unitary spaces and the canonical forms of operators. It is terse, it is thorough, and it is one of the cheapest serious mathematics books in print. Use it when Axler’s elegance leaves us wanting more concrete computation, or when we simply want a second explanation of a hard section.

Review to follow.

Vector Calculus, Linear Algebra, and Differential Forms: A Unified Approach, John H. Hubbard, Barbara Burke Hubbard. 5th ed., Matrix Editions, 2015.

The Hubbards refuse to separate linear algebra from multivariable calculus, and once we have read the book it is hard to see why anyone does. Matrices appear because derivatives of functions of several variables are linear maps, and the whole book builds towards differential forms and the general Stokes’ theorem. It is unusually honest about rigour and about computation at once. The inverse and implicit function theorems, for instance, are proved through Newton’s method, which turns an abstract existence result into an algorithm. It is long and demanding, and it makes a natural bridge from Phase 1 into manifolds.

Review to follow.

Algebra I: Textbook for Students of Mathematics, Alexey L. Gorodentsev. 1st ed., Springer, 2016.

Gorodentsev’s two volumes come from the intensive algebra course at the Independent University of Moscow, and they cover in two years what many universities spread over four. Volume I runs from integers, polynomials and fields through vector spaces, duality, matrices, bilinear forms and projective geometry to a first look at groups and modules. The style is compact and the problems are hard, in the Russian tradition. This is not a gentle first book. It is the book for students who have already met linear algebra once and want to see how much further it goes.

Review to follow.

Statistics 1 (Probability)

Introduction to Probability, Joseph K. Blitzstein, Jessica Hwang. 2nd ed., CRC Press, 2019.

Blitzstein and Hwang teach probability the way it is actually used. Every idea comes with a ‘story’ that explains what it models, many results are proved with clever counting arguments rather than heavy algebra, and each chapter ends with R code so we can simulate what we just proved. The book grew out of Harvard’s Stat 110, and the full set of lectures and practice problems with solutions is free online. It stops short of measure theory, which is exactly right for a first course. Start here, and come back to Feller later for depth.

Review to follow. · Free online

An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its Applications, Vol. I, William Feller. 3rd ed., Wiley, 1968.

Feller’s first volume never uses measure theory, yet it goes deeper into discrete probability than almost anything written since. Its chapter on fluctuations in coin tossing, with the arc sine law, overturns most of what we think we know about luck. Random walks, generating functions, recurrent events and Markov chains are all treated by a master. The notation is old-fashioned and the pace is not gentle. Read it after Blitzstein and Hwang, slowly, and with a pencil.

Review to follow.

Computing

CS50x: Introduction to Computer Science, David J. Malan et al.. Online course, Harvard University.

Harvard’s introductory computer science course, free online and taught by David Malan with remarkable energy. It begins with Scratch, spends several weeks in C, where we learn how memory and pointers actually work, and then moves on to Python, SQL and the web. It is not mathematics, but the problem sets teach the same precision a proof demands, and few things humble us faster than a program that refuses to do what we were sure we told it to do.

Review to follow. · Free online

A Primer on Scientific Programming with Python, Hans Petter Langtangen. 5th ed., Springer (TCSE), 2016.

Langtangen wrote this for science students, not programmers, and every example is a scientific computation rather than a toy. The book starts from scratch with variables and loops and builds up to functions, files, classes, numerical arrays, plotting and simple simulations of differential equations, with the object-oriented ideas introduced only when they solve a real problem. If we want to check a series numerically, plot a phase portrait or simulate a random walk from Feller, this is how we learn to do it.

Review to follow.

Phase 2 (Intermediate)

Once the foundations are in, the subjects start talking to each other. Calculus grows up into analysis on manifolds, algebra turns abstract, topology shows up in its own right, differential equations become dynamical systems and probability becomes statistical inference. Think of this as the second and third year of a good undergraduate programme, the kind most of us were never actually offered.

Analysis 2 (Manifolds)

Vector Calculus, Linear Algebra, and Differential Forms: A Unified Approach, John H. Hubbard, Barbara Burke Hubbard. 5th ed., Matrix Editions, 2015.

The Hubbards refuse to separate linear algebra from multivariable calculus, and once we have read the book it is hard to see why anyone does. Matrices appear because derivatives of functions of several variables are linear maps, and the whole book builds towards differential forms and the general Stokes’ theorem. It is unusually honest about rigour and about computation at once. The inverse and implicit function theorems, for instance, are proved through Newton’s method, which turns an abstract existence result into an algorithm. It is long and demanding, and it makes a natural bridge from Phase 1 into manifolds.

Review to follow.

Basic Analysis II: Introduction to Real Analysis, Volume II, Jiří Lebl. Version 6.3, self-published, 2026.

The second volume carries on exactly where the first stops, even down to the chapter numbers. It covers differential calculus in several variables up to the inverse and implicit function theorems, then two topics many courses skip, differentiation under the integral sign and path integrals, before the multivariable Riemann integral and a basic case of Green’s theorem. A final chapter treats power series, Arzelà–Ascoli, Stone–Weierstrass and Fourier series. Lebl says the first volume plus the first three chapters of this one prepare us for graduate real and complex analysis. It stays in Euclidean space, so Hubbard and Tu take over for differential forms and manifolds.

Review to follow. · Free online

An Introduction to Manifolds, Loring W. Tu. 2nd ed., Springer (Universitext), 2011.

Tu wrote this as the prerequisite for Bott and Tu’s famous book on differential forms in algebraic topology, and it is designed to need almost nothing beyond calculus and linear algebra. Every definition is motivated by an example in Euclidean space before being generalised, every proof is complete, and the book builds steadily from manifolds and tangent spaces through vector fields, Lie groups and differential forms to Stokes’ theorem and de Rham cohomology. It is about as gentle as a serious introduction to manifolds can be, and a new edition is on its way.

Review to follow.

Mathematical Analysis II, Vladimir A. Zorich. 2nd ed., Springer (Universitext), 2016.

The second volume is where Zorich really separates himself from the competition. It moves to metric and normed spaces, then redoes differential calculus in that general setting, before covering multiple integrals, surfaces, differential forms, line and surface integrals and integration on manifolds, and finally series of functions, integrals depending on a parameter, Fourier series and the Fourier transform, and asymptotic expansions. Physicists will recognise vector analysis and field theory treated properly for once. Use it alongside Tu or Hubbard, and keep it as the reference for everything analysis has to say about calculus.

Review to follow.

Algebra 2 (Abstract)

Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications, Thomas W. Judson. 2026 edition, Orthogonal Publishing, 2026.

Judson’s book is free, open-source and updated regularly, and it is a serious undergraduate algebra text in its own right. It goes from groups through rings and fields to Galois theory, and stands out for its applications chapters on cryptography, coding theory and Gröbner bases, which show algebra doing real work. The online version includes Sage exercises we can run in the browser. Use it as a free main text or as a second voice next to Silverman or Artin.

Review to follow. · Free online

Abstract Algebra: An Integrated Approach, Joseph H. Silverman. 1st ed., AMS (Pure and Applied Undergraduate Texts 55), 2022.

Most algebra courses teach groups for a semester, then rings, then fields, as if they had nothing to do with one another. Silverman develops all three side by side, so that cosets and ideals, quotient groups and quotient rings, appear together and illuminate each other. The book covers a full year of undergraduate algebra with exercises after every section, and it is written in a clear and friendly voice. It works very well as a main text.

Review to follow.

Algebra, Michael Artin. 2nd ed., Pearson, 2011.

Artin’s algebra is built around linear algebra and symmetry, which makes it ideal for anyone heading towards physics. Groups appear first as matrix groups and as symmetries of plane figures, group actions do much of the heavy lifting, and there are whole chapters on linear groups (SU(2) and the rotation group) and on group representations that most algebra texts leave out. The second half covers rings, factorisation, modules and Galois theory. It is opinionated and occasionally idiosyncratic, but few algebra books make the subject feel so concrete.

Review to follow.

Algebra I: Textbook for Students of Mathematics, Alexey L. Gorodentsev. 1st ed., Springer, 2016.

Gorodentsev’s two volumes come from the intensive algebra course at the Independent University of Moscow, and they cover in two years what many universities spread over four. Volume I runs from integers, polynomials and fields through vector spaces, duality, matrices, bilinear forms and projective geometry to a first look at groups and modules. The style is compact and the problems are hard, in the Russian tradition. This is not a gentle first book. It is the book for students who have already met linear algebra once and want to see how much further it goes.

Review to follow.

Topology 1 (General)

Introduction to Topology, Bert Mendelson. 3rd ed., Dover, 1990.

Mendelson is a slim Dover book that eases us into topology from familiar ground. It starts with metric spaces, which we already know from analysis, uses them to motivate topological spaces, and then covers connectedness and compactness before finishing with homotopy and the fundamental group. It does far less than Munkres, but it is short enough to finish in a few weeks, which makes it a good first pass before the real thing.

Review to follow.

Topology, James R. Munkres. 2nd ed., Prentice Hall / Pearson, 2000.

Munkres is the book most of us learn topology from, and it deserves its reputation. Part I treats point-set topology with great care, covering connectedness, compactness, the countability and separation axioms, Urysohn’s lemma, the metrisation theorems and Tychonoff’s theorem. Part II introduces the fundamental group, covering spaces, the Seifert–van Kampen theorem and the classification of surfaces. The exercises are excellent and the exposition never skips a step. It works well as the main text, with Jänich nearby for the pictures.

Review to follow.

Topology, Klaus Jänich. 1st ed. English, Springer (UTM), 1984.

Jänich’s book is short, informal and full of drawings, and it answers the question most topology books never ask, namely what topologists actually picture when they talk. It explains why quotient spaces, homotopy, CW complexes and covering spaces matter before (or instead of) proving everything about them. It will not replace Munkres, but read alongside Munkres it makes the abstract definitions feel alive.

Review to follow.

Topology, Stefan Friedl. Lecture notes, AMS Open Math Notes, 2023.

Stefan Friedl’s topology notes are freely available through the AMS and run to thousands of pages, which makes them a reference rather than a textbook. They are the place to look when Munkres or Hatcher skips a step. Use the table of contents to jump straight to what we need.

Review to follow. · Free online

Counterexamples in Topology, Lynn Arthur Steen, J. Arthur Seebach Jr.. Dover reprint of 2nd, Dover, 1995.

This is not a textbook but a catalogue of 143 topological spaces, many of them deeply strange, together with tables showing which spaces satisfy which properties. Its purpose is to answer the question every topology student eventually asks, which is whether one property really implies another or whether there is a counterexample. Keep it next to Munkres and look up the relevant space whenever a definition starts to feel obvious. It usually is not.

Review to follow.

Introduction to Topological Manifolds, John M. Lee. 2nd ed., Springer (GTM 202), 2011.

Lee teaches general and algebraic topology with manifolds as the motivating example throughout, which gives every abstract idea a reason to exist. The book covers the point-set topology a geometer actually needs, cell complexes, the fundamental group, covering spaces, the classification of surfaces and an introduction to singular homology. Lee’s writing is exceptionally clear and his exercises are well chosen. It is the natural bridge between Munkres and Lee’s own smooth manifolds book in Phase 3.

Review to follow.

Differential Equations 1 (ODE)

Differential Equations, Dynamical Systems, and an Introduction to Chaos, Morris W. Hirsch, Stephen Smale, Robert L. Devaney. 3rd ed., Academic Press, 2013.

The original Hirsch and Smale of 1974 was famous for treating differential equations as dynamical systems, and this edition, rewritten with Devaney, keeps that spirit while being far more applied. Instead of a catalogue of solution techniques, we get linear systems understood through their eigenvalues, phase portraits, stability, bifurcations, and eventually the Lorenz system and chaos, with applications to predator–prey models, epidemics and circuits. It is the book to read after years of being told to recognise the equation and apply the trick.

Review to follow.

Ordinary Differential Equations, Vladimir I. Arnold. 3rd ed. (transl. Cooke), Springer (Universitext), 1992.

Arnold’s book on ordinary differential equations is unlike any other. There are almost no recipes for solving equations. Instead, an equation is a vector field, a solution is a curve following it, and the theorems on existence, uniqueness, dependence on initial conditions and stability are all statements about geometry. The pictures are wonderful and the problems are thought-provoking. Read it alongside Hirsch, Smale and Devaney, who supply the examples, while Arnold supplies the way of thinking.

Review to follow.

Statistics 2 (Inference)

Introduction to Mathematical Statistics, Robert V. Hogg, Joseph W. McKean, Allen T. Craig. 8th ed., Pearson, 2019.

Hogg, McKean and Craig has been teaching mathematical statistics since 1959, and the current edition covers much of the same ground as Casella and Berger at a gentler, undergraduate pace. It includes sections on nonparametric and Bayesian methods and on linear models. Use it as the first pass through statistical theory, and move to Casella and Berger when it starts to feel easy.

Review to follow.

All of Statistics, Larry Wasserman. 1st ed., Springer, 2004.

Wasserman wrote this for graduate students in computer science and machine learning who needed statistics quickly, and it covers in about 400 pages what usually takes several courses. It moves from probability through the bootstrap, nonparametric methods, Bayesian inference, graphical models and causal inference. The price of that speed is terseness. As its MAA reviewer pointed out, it is not ideal for self-study, so use it as a map of modern statistics and fill in the details from Casella and Berger.

Review to follow. · Free online

Statistical Inference, George Casella, Roger L. Berger. 2nd ed. (CRC reprint), Chapman & Hall/CRC, 2024.

Casella and Berger is the standard first-year graduate text in statistical theory, and the book that most qualifying exams are written from. The first half develops probability and distribution theory carefully, and the second half treats sufficiency, the likelihood principle, point estimation, hypothesis testing, interval estimation and asymptotics, ending with ANOVA and regression. The exercises are numerous and excellent. It is demanding, so we should be comfortable with calculus and Phase 1 probability before starting.

Review to follow.

Phase 3 (Advanced)

Welcome to graduate mathematics, which also happens to be where the physics starts showing through. Measure theory gives probability and quantum mechanics their footing, complex analysis and representation theory become everyday tools, partial differential equations move to centre stage, and differential geometry hands us the language that modern physics is written in. Each book here is a matter of months rather than weeks, so let us be kind to ourselves.

Analysis 3 (Measures)

Measure, Integration & Real Analysis, Sheldon Axler. 1st ed., Springer (GTM 282), 2020.

Axler’s measure theory book does for real analysis what his linear algebra book does for linear algebra. It strips the subject back to its main ideas and presents them with unusual clarity. It covers Lebesgue measure and integration, product measures, $L^p$ spaces, Hilbert and Banach spaces, the spectral theorem for compact operators, Fourier analysis and a closing chapter on probability. That makes it a single route from the Lebesgue integral to the mathematics of quantum mechanics. It is open access, and the colour-coded definition and theorem boxes make it very easy to navigate. It makes a natural first text for the subject.

Review to follow. · Free online

Introductory Real Analysis, A. N. Kolmogorov, S. V. Fomin. Dover (transl. Silverman), Dover, 1975.

Kolmogorov and Fomin wrote the Moscow course that taught generations to think of analysis in terms of spaces and operators. The book begins with metric and topological spaces, moves to normed spaces, linear functionals and operators, and only then develops measure, the Lebesgue integral and $L^2$, ending with integral equations. Measure theory is treated briefly and there are few examples, but the functional-analytic viewpoint is introduced earlier and more naturally than in any Western text. Read it after Axler for a different angle on the same material.

Review to follow. · Free online

Probability and Measure, Patrick Billingsley. Anniversary, Wiley, 2012.

Billingsley develops measure theory and probability together, and the probability motivates the measure theory rather than the other way round. The book famously opens with Borel’s normal number theorem and with gambling systems, so we see why we need countable additivity before we are asked to accept it. From there it covers integration, random variables, convergence in distribution, conditional probability and expectation, and stochastic processes. The examples and exercises are among the best in any probability book. For anyone whose real interest is probability, this is the measure theory book to read.

Review to follow. · Free online

Lectures on Quantum Theory, Frederic P. Schuller (lectures); Simon Rea (notes). Lecture course, FAU Erlangen–Nürnberg, 2015.

Schuller’s 2015 lectures at Erlangen take a radical approach. They start from the axioms of quantum mechanics and then develop, in full, the mathematics needed to make sense of them. That means Banach and Hilbert spaces, measure theory and the Lebesgue integral, self-adjoint operators and their domains, and the spectral theorem, before returning to physics with spin, composite systems, the harmonic oscillator and periodic potentials. We finally see why physicists’ casual manipulations of unbounded operators can go wrong. The written notes are by Simon Rea, based on the recorded lectures.

Review to follow. · Free online

Real Analysis, H. L. Royden, P. M. Fitzpatrick. 5th ed., Pearson, 2023.

Royden has been the standard first-year graduate analysis text for decades, and the fifth edition, revised by Fitzpatrick, greatly expands the material on $L^p$ spaces, Banach spaces and linear operators. The book is organised in three parts, covering Lebesgue integration on the real line, then abstract metric, topological and Banach spaces, and finally general measure and integration theory. It is terse and short on examples, as its MAA reviewer complained, but it is thorough and has enough exercises for three semesters. Use Axler to learn and Royden to drill.

Review to follow.

Principles of Analysis: Measure, Integration, Functional Analysis, and Applications, Hugo D. Junghenn. 1st ed. (pbk 2023), CRC Press, 2018.

Junghenn wrote this for PhD students preparing for qualifying exams, and it reads like a carefully organised course. It covers metric and topological preliminaries, Lebesgue integration and Fourier analysis, measures on locally compact spaces, Banach, Hilbert and locally convex spaces, Banach algebras, and applications to harmonic analysis, probability and operator theory, with nearly 700 exercises. It is less elegant than Axler and less complete than Bogachev, but it is an excellent source of practice problems at this level.

Review to follow.

Functional Analysis, Frigyes Riesz, Béla Sz.-Nagy. Dover reprint, Dover, 1990.

Riesz and Sz.-Nagy wrote this in the early 1950s, when functional analysis was still being built, and Riesz was one of its architects. The first part develops differentiation and integration from scratch, using Riesz’s own elegant methods. The rest covers integral equations, completely continuous operators and the spectral theory of bounded and unbounded self-adjoint operators, which is precisely the mathematics behind quantum mechanics. There are no exercises and some terminology is dated, but no modern book explains these ideas with such clarity or such a sense of where they came from.

Review to follow.

Measure Theory, Vol. I, Vladimir I. Bogachev. 1st ed., Springer, 2007.

Bogachev’s two volumes are the most complete treatment of measure theory in existence. Every result we might need is somewhere in them, along with extensive historical and bibliographical commentary and an enormous collection of exercises with hints. Volume I covers the construction and extension of measures, the Lebesgue integral, product measures, $L^p$ spaces and the relation between the integral and the derivative. It is not a first text. Use it when Axler or Royden leaves a gap, or when we need the strongest version of a theorem.

Review to follow.

Measure Theory, Vol. II, Vladimir I. Bogachev. 1st ed., Springer, 2007.

Volume II moves beyond the usual graduate course into Borel, Baire and Souslin sets, measures on topological spaces, weak convergence of measures, transformations and isomorphisms of measures, and conditional measures and expectations. This is the material probability theory and mathematical physics actually depend on, and it is hard to find collected anywhere else. The bibliographical notes alone are worth the price.

Review to follow.

Foundations of Modern Probability, Olav Kallenberg. 3rd ed., Springer (Probability Theory and Stochastic Modelling 99), 2021.

Kallenberg’s book aims to cover all of modern probability theory in one place, from measure-theoretic foundations through martingales, Brownian motion, Lévy processes, Markov processes and stochastic calculus to his own specialities such as random measures and exchangeability. The third edition is so large that its print version comes in two volumes. The writing is famously compressed, with every proof reduced to its essentials. It is not a book to learn from first. It is the reference we graduate into after Billingsley, and the one we keep on the desk for the rest of our lives.

Review to follow.

Analysis 4 (Complex)

Complex Analysis, Elias M. Stein, Rami Shakarchi. 1st ed., Princeton University Press, 2003.

This is the second of the four Princeton Lectures in Analysis, and its great strength is that complex analysis is constantly connected to the rest of mathematics. Alongside the standard Cauchy theory, it uses complex methods for the Fourier transform, develops entire functions, the gamma and zeta functions, proves the prime number theorem, and ends with elliptic functions and theta functions applied to the problem of writing numbers as sums of squares. The exercises come with helpful hints. It makes a very rewarding first rigorous course.

Review to follow.

Complex Variables: Introduction and Applications, Mark J. Ablowitz, Athanassios S. Fokas. 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2003.

Ablowitz and Fokas wrote complex analysis for scientists and engineers who need to use it, and that makes it exactly what physicists want. The first part covers the standard theory efficiently. The second part is the real reason to read the book, with asymptotic evaluation of integrals by steepest descent, conformal mapping applied to fluid flow, and Riemann–Hilbert and Wiener–Hopf problems. These techniques appear constantly in physics papers and almost never in mathematics courses.

Review to follow.

Complex Analysis, Donald E. Marshall. 1st ed., Cambridge University Press, 2019.

Marshall builds complex analysis from power series, in the tradition of Weierstrass, rather than from Cauchy’s integral theorem, which gives the subject a very concrete feel. The book starts with an engaging historical introduction and goes well beyond a first course, through normal families and conformal mapping to two different proofs of the Riemann mapping theorem. The exercises are graded A, B and C by difficulty, with hints, which makes it particularly good for working on our own.

Review to follow.

Complex Analysis, Lars V. Ahlfors. 3rd ed. (AMS Chelsea reprint), AMS Chelsea, 2021.

Ahlfors was one of the first two Fields medallists, and his textbook reads like it, concise, elegant and without a wasted word. It covers complex functions and conformality, Cauchy’s theorem in its general form, series and product expansions, harmonic functions and the Dirichlet problem, the Riemann mapping theorem, elliptic functions and global analytic functions. Ahlfors assumes a mature reader and leaves much to the exercises, so it is hard going alone. Read Stein and Shakarchi first, then Ahlfors to see how a master thinks.

Review to follow.

A Course in Complex Analysis, Saeed Zakeri. 1st ed., Princeton University Press, 2021.

Zakeri’s course is modern, geometric and beautifully written, with historical notes running down the margins. Its distinctive feature is that it builds up to the uniformisation theorem, which most textbooks only mention, and treats the hyperbolic and topological sides of the subject seriously. As its MAA reviewer said, it is not for beginners. It assumes a solid background in topology and analysis, and the exercises are hard and have no solutions. Read it after a first course for a glimpse of where complex analysis is heading.

Review to follow.

Algebra 3 (Representation Theory)

Representation Theory of Finite Groups: An Introductory Approach, Benjamin Steinberg. 1st ed., Springer (Universitext), 2012.

Steinberg’s book needs nothing more than linear algebra and basic group theory, and it avoids modules and tensor products entirely. Representations of finite groups are treated as Fourier analysis on the group, which makes the theory feel natural rather than mysterious, and the applications range from random walks on groups to spectral graph theory. It is short and clear, and very well suited to self-study.

Review to follow.

Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and Representations, Brian C. Hall. 2nd ed., Springer (GTM 222), 2015.

Most books on Lie groups demand differential geometry first. Hall works only with matrix groups, which means linear algebra and some analysis are enough to reach Lie algebras, the exponential map and the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula. He then works through $\mathfrak{sl}(3,\mathbb{C})$ in full detail before the general theory, so that root systems and the Weyl character formula arrive with a concrete example already in hand. It is especially well suited to physicists, who meet SU(2), SU(3) and the Lorentz group long before they meet manifolds.

Review to follow.

Algebra II: Textbook for Students of Mathematics, Alexey L. Gorodentsev. 1st ed., Springer, 2017.

The second volume of Gorodentsev’s course covers modules, tensor and multilinear algebra, representation theory, categories, Galois theory, commutative algebra and the beginnings of algebraic geometry. It is listed here for its representation theory, but it is really a panorama of the algebra a working mathematician needs. Its MAA reviewer found it terse, light on examples and written for mathematicians rather than students, which is fair. Use it after Steinberg and Hall, when we want to see representation theory in its wider algebraic setting.

Review to follow.

Differential Equations 2 (PDE)

Partial Differential Equations: A First Course, Rustum Choksi. 1st ed., AMS (Pure and Applied Undergraduate Texts 54), 2022.

Most first courses in partial differential equations amount to separation of variables and Fourier series. Choksi takes the Dirac delta as a unifying theme and builds a genuine theory of distributions, so that Green’s functions, fundamental solutions and the Fourier transform all make proper sense. The book is clear and modular, with many exercises, and its examples lean heavily on physics. It is the right first book for anyone who wants to understand, rather than merely solve, the equations of mathematical physics.

Review to follow.

Partial Differential Equations, Lawrence C. Evans. 2nd ed., AMS (GSM 19), 2010.

Evans is the standard graduate text on partial differential equations. Part I covers representation formulae for the classical linear equations and first-order nonlinear equations. Part II develops Sobolev spaces and the theory of second-order elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic equations. Part III treats the calculus of variations, Hamilton–Jacobi equations and conservation laws. It is comprehensive and surprisingly approachable, with many exercises, although its size can be intimidating. Read Choksi first, then Evans.

Review to follow.

Lectures on Partial Differential Equations, Vladimir I. Arnold. 1st ed. English, Springer, 2004.

Arnold’s twelve lectures on partial differential equations are short, unconventional and full of geometric and physical insight. Rather than building a systematic theory, they explore the Laplace, wave and heat equations and related problems, always asking why things are the way they are. They end with problems from Arnold’s own Moscow exams. Read them alongside Evans to see the subject through the eyes of one of the great mathematicians of the twentieth century.

Geometry 1 (Differential)

Lectures on the Geometric Anatomy of Theoretical Physics, Frederic P. Schuller (lectures); Simon Rea (notes). Lecture course, FAU Erlangen–Nürnberg, 2013–14.

Schuller’s Erlangen lectures build the mathematics of theoretical physics from the ground up. They begin with logic, set theory and topology, then develop manifolds, tensors, Lie groups and Lie algebras, principal and associated bundles, connections and curvature, and end with quantum mechanics on curved spaces and spin structures. Nothing is waved away. Every structure is defined before it is used, and every definition is motivated by the physics it will eventually support. The written notes are by Simon Rea, based on the recorded lectures.

Review to follow. · Free online

Introduction to Smooth Manifolds, John M. Lee. 2nd ed., Springer (GTM 218), 2012.

Lee’s book is the standard graduate text on smooth manifolds. Over 700 pages it covers tangent and cotangent bundles, submanifolds, Sard’s theorem and transversality, vector bundles, tensors, differential forms and integration, de Rham cohomology, Lie groups and their actions, flows, foliations and an introduction to symplectic geometry. The writing is careful and clear, and the exercises are excellent. It assumes a course in topology at the level of Lee’s own topological manifolds book, and it leads naturally into his Riemannian geometry.

Review to follow.

A Comprehensive Introduction to Differential Geometry, Vol. 1, Michael Spivak. 3rd ed., Publish or Perish, 1999.

Spivak’s five-volume work is one of the great textbooks of mathematics, partly because of its ambition and partly because of its voice. He is chatty, funny and opinionated, and he never lets us forget why the definitions are the way they are. Volume 1 covers the foundations, namely manifolds, vector bundles, tensors, differential forms, integration and de Rham cohomology, Lie groups and Riemannian metrics. Use it as a companion to Lee when we want a second, more discursive explanation.

Review to follow.

Differential Geometry: Connections, Curvature, and Characteristic Classes, Loring W. Tu. 1st ed., Springer (GTM 275), 2017.

Tu’s sequel to his manifolds book is a concise route from Gauss’s Theorema Egregium and the Gauss–Bonnet theorem to connections and curvature on vector bundles and principal bundles, and finally the Chern–Weil theory of characteristic classes. It was written for both mathematics and physics students, and its treatment of connections on principal bundles is exactly what gauge theory needs. It is short enough to read in a semester, which is rare for this material.

Review to follow.

A Comprehensive Introduction to Differential Geometry, Vol. 2, Michael Spivak. 3rd ed., Publish or Perish, 1999.

Volume 2 is where the geometry begins, and it is unique among textbooks in teaching the subject historically. Spivak includes translations of Gauss’s paper on curved surfaces and Riemann’s inaugural lecture on the foundations of geometry, and then shows how each of their ideas becomes a modern definition of connections and curvature. Reading Gauss and Riemann in their own words, with Spivak as our guide, is an unforgettable experience.

Review to follow.

A Comprehensive Introduction to Differential Geometry, Vol. 3, Michael Spivak. 3rd ed., Publish or Perish, 1999.

Volume 3 returns to classical differential geometry, covering curves and surfaces in Euclidean space and the classical theory of surfaces, all seen with the machinery of the first two volumes. It is the clearest illustration of how much modern geometry is already hiding in the classical theory of surfaces.

Phase 4 (Specialised) · Pure Mathematics

By now there is a choice to make, and most of us will happily refuse to make it. The pure mathematics shelf covers algebraic geometry, algebraic topology, category theory and number theory. The theoretical physics shelf runs from classical particles and fields through quantum systems and quantum field theory to statistical physics. Quite a few books here are research-level references, so treat them as places to keep coming back to rather than books to finish.

These are the parts of pure mathematics that the rest of mathematics keeps quietly borrowing from.

Geometry 2 (Algebraic)

Algebraic Curves: An Introduction to Algebraic Geometry, William Fulton. 3rd ed. (free 2008 version), self-published (orig. Addison-Wesley), 2008.

Fulton’s book on algebraic curves is short (around 120 pages) and remains a standard introduction to the classical theory. It covers affine and projective plane curves, intersection numbers and Bézout’s theorem, Max Noether’s fundamental theorem, resolution of singularities and the Riemann–Roch theorem, with several hundred problems. It needs only a semester of abstract algebra. Fulton has made the latest edition freely available.

Review to follow. · Free online

Algebraic Geometry: A Problem Solving Approach, Thomas Garrity et al.. 1st ed., AMS (Student Mathematical Library 66), 2013.

This book teaches algebraic geometry almost entirely through problems. It begins with conics, which a strong high school student can follow, moves through cubic curves and the group law on elliptic curves, then higher-degree curves and varieties, and ends with a first look at sheaves and cohomology. Each section states its goals clearly and the annotated bibliography is a fine guide to further reading. There are no solutions, so it works best in a study group, and it is a friendly way into a notoriously forbidding subject.

Review to follow.

An Invitation to Algebraic Geometry, Karen E. Smith et al.. 1st ed., Springer (Universitext), 2000.

Smith and her co-authors wrote a short book whose purpose is to show beginners what algebraic geometry is about and where it is going. It assumes little beyond linear algebra and some ring theory, explains varieties, smoothness, birational geometry and maps to projective space informally, and constantly connects elementary ideas to current research. It does not prove everything, nor does it try to. Read it before Bosch or Fulton to get a sense of the landscape.

Review to follow.

Algebraic Geometry and Commutative Algebra, Siegfried Bosch. 2nd ed., Springer (Universitext), 2022.

Bosch develops commutative algebra and algebraic geometry together, so that every algebraic tool (localisation, integral extensions, dimension theory) is introduced just before it is needed geometrically. The book ends with Grothendieck’s schemes, treated rigorously and with detailed proofs. The second edition is a careful revision of the first. It is the most self-contained route to modern algebraic geometry, and gentler than the standard references.

Review to follow.

Topology 2 (Algebraic)

Algebraic Topology, Allen Hatcher. 1st ed., Cambridge University Press, 2002.

Hatcher’s book has become the standard first course in algebraic topology, and its strongly geometric style is the reason. Ideas are motivated by pictures and examples before they are formalised. It covers the fundamental group and covering spaces, simplicial, singular and cellular homology, cohomology, cup products and Poincaré duality, and an introduction to homotopy theory, with many optional sections on further topics. The proofs are sometimes informal, so it is worth keeping Lee or Friedl nearby for the details. Hatcher keeps it free on his website.

Review to follow. · Free online

Topology, Stefan Friedl. Lecture notes, AMS Open Math Notes, 2023.

Stefan Friedl’s topology notes are freely available through the AMS and run to thousands of pages, which makes them a reference rather than a textbook. They are the place to look when Munkres or Hatcher skips a step. Use the table of contents to jump straight to what we need.

Review to follow. · Free online

Basic Algebraic Topology, Anant R. Shastri. 1st ed., CRC Press, 2013.

Anant Shastri of IIT Bombay wrote a full year of algebraic topology that goes much further than most first texts. After the fundamental group, covering spaces, homology and cohomology, it covers manifolds and Poincaré duality, de Rham theory, higher homotopy, obstruction theory, fibrations, characteristic classes and spectral sequences, with historical remarks throughout. Its MAA reviewer admired how much wonderful material it covers, while warning that it covers it tersely. Use it after Hatcher as the next step towards research.

Review to follow.

Foundations 2 (Categories)

Conceptual Mathematics: A First Introduction to Categories, F. William Lawvere, Stephen H. Schanuel. 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2009.

Lawvere and Schanuel wrote an introduction to category theory that assumes only school algebra. Using sets and maps, they develop composition, isomorphisms, universal properties and eventually toposes, with dialogues between teacher and students that anticipate our confusions. It looks simple, and it is, but it also gives a deep sense of why category theory is a natural way to think about mathematics. Read it first, and then move to Riehl.

Review to follow.

Category Theory in Context, Emily Riehl. 1st ed., Dover, 2016.

Riehl’s book is a first serious course in category theory. It covers categories, functors and natural transformations, universal properties and the Yoneda lemma, limits and colimits, adjunctions, monads and Kan extensions, and almost every idea is illustrated with examples from algebra and topology that we already know from earlier phases. The chapters on monads and Kan extensions are particularly clear. Riehl keeps the book free on her website.

Review to follow. · Free online

Algebra 4 (Numbers)

Algebraic Number Theory, James S. Milne. Course notes, v3.08, self-published, 2020.

Milne’s course notes on algebraic number theory are free, regularly updated and about as clear as course notes get. They cover rings of integers, Dedekind domains, the finiteness of the class number, the unit theorem, cyclotomic fields and Fermat’s last theorem for regular primes, valuations and local fields, and global fields, with solutions to the exercises at the end. They assume some commutative algebra. This is the right place to start algebraic number theory, before Weil.

Review to follow. · Free online

Basic Number Theory, André Weil. 3rd ed., Springer (Classics in Mathematics), 1995.

Weil’s book treats algebraic number theory and class field theory uniformly for number fields and function fields, which he calls A-fields, through locally compact fields, adeles, ideles and zeta functions. Class field theory is developed by way of simple algebras, an approach found almost nowhere else. The style is famously austere. It is not for beginners, but once we are comfortable with Milne, Weil shows how the whole subject fits together.

Phase 4 (Specialised) · Theoretical Physics

By now there is a choice to make, and most of us will happily refuse to make it. The pure mathematics shelf covers algebraic geometry, algebraic topology, category theory and number theory. The theoretical physics shelf runs from classical particles and fields through quantum systems and quantum field theory to statistical physics. Quite a few books here are research-level references, so treat them as places to keep coming back to rather than books to finish.

Five subjects, in roughly the order in which the physics itself grew up. None of the mathematics from the earlier phases is optional here, which is rather the point of having read it.

Physics 1 (Classical Particles)

Lectures on the Geometric Anatomy of Theoretical Physics, Frederic P. Schuller (lectures); Simon Rea (notes). Lecture course, FAU Erlangen–Nürnberg, 2013–14.

Schuller’s Erlangen lectures build the mathematics of theoretical physics from the ground up. They begin with logic, set theory and topology, then develop manifolds, tensors, Lie groups and Lie algebras, principal and associated bundles, connections and curvature, and end with quantum mechanics on curved spaces and spin structures. Nothing is waved away. Every structure is defined before it is used, and every definition is motivated by the physics it will eventually support. The written notes are by Simon Rea, based on the recorded lectures.

Review to follow. · Free online

Physics for Mathematicians: Mechanics I, Michael Spivak. 1st ed., Publish or Perish, 2010.

Spivak wrote this for mathematicians who want to understand classical mechanics, and it answers all the questions that physics courses never address. What exactly is a force? Why does the principle of least action work? How should we think about rigid bodies and constraints? It goes from Newton’s laws through rigid body motion, Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics to Hamilton–Jacobi theory, rigorously and with Spivak’s usual humour.

Review to follow.

Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, Vladimir I. Arnold. 2nd ed., Springer (GTM 60), 1989.

Arnold’s book is how a generation of physicists should have learned classical mechanics. For the most part they did not, and most still do not, which is a real shame. Newtonian mechanics leads to Lagrangian mechanics on manifolds, which leads to Hamiltonian mechanics as symplectic geometry, with differential forms introduced along the way as the natural language. The appendices go further still, into Riemannian curvature, the motion of rigid bodies as geodesics on Lie groups, and perturbation theory. Read it after a standard mechanics course and the differential geometry of Phase 3. Then we will never see a Poisson bracket the same way again.

Review to follow.

Classical Mathematical Physics: Dynamical Systems and Field Theories, Walter Thirring. 3rd ed., Springer, 1997.

Thirring wrote a four-volume course in mathematical physics, and this book combines the first two volumes on classical dynamical systems and classical field theory. It is rigorous and written throughout in the language of differential forms, and it includes topics most mechanics books omit, such as scattering theory and relativistic motion, including orbits in the Schwarzschild geometry. One reviewer called it a mathematical physicist’s Landau and Lifshitz, which captures both its scope and its density.

Review to follow.

Physics 2 (Classical Fields)

Lectures on Gravity and Light, Frederic P. Schuller (lectures). Lecture course, WE-Heraeus International Winter School on Gravity and Light, Linz, 2015.

Schuller’s lectures from the 2015 WE-Heraeus winter school in Linz build general relativity from first principles, starting with topology and manifolds and developing bundles, connections and curvature before reaching Einstein’s equations, the Schwarzschild solution and cosmology. The lectures were accompanied by tutorial problems, and the whole course is freely available. For students who want to see general relativity done with full mathematical honesty, this is the place to start.

Review to follow. · Free online

Electricity and Magnetism for Mathematicians, Thomas A. Garrity. 1st ed., Cambridge University Press, 2015.

Garrity uses Maxwell’s equations as a gateway to a surprising amount of modern mathematics. Starting from the equations in vector calculus form, he develops special relativity, differential forms and the Hodge star, gauge theory, connections and curvature, and finally Yang–Mills theory. The book is short and brisk, as its MAA reviewer noted, and assumes fluency in vector calculus. It makes a natural bridge from the physics of Phase 0 to the geometry of Phase 3.

Review to follow.

A Mathematical Introduction to General Relativity, Amol Sasane. 2nd ed., World Scientific, 2025.

Sasane wrote a proof-based introduction to general relativity for mathematics undergraduates. Part I develops differential geometry from scratch, using Lorentzian examples throughout. Part II covers the energy–momentum tensor, Einstein’s equation, the Newtonian limit, geodesics, black holes and cosmology. Its great virtue is that all of its exercises come with full solutions, which makes it ideal for self-study. The second edition adds new exercises and fixes the slips of the first.

Foundations of Classical Electrodynamics: Charge, Flux, and Metric, Friedrich W. Hehl, Yuri N. Obukhov. 1st ed., Birkhäuser, 2003.

Hehl and Obukhov rebuild classical electrodynamics from a small set of axioms based on conservation of charge and magnetic flux, using exterior calculus throughout. The metric of spacetime enters only at the very end, through the constitutive law relating fields to excitations. The result shows precisely which parts of Maxwell’s theory depend on geometry and which do not. It is graduate-level and demanding, but it gives the clearest picture of the logical structure of electromagnetism available.

Semi-Riemannian Geometry with Applications to Relativity, Barrett O'Neill. 1st ed., Academic Press, 1983.

O’Neill develops semi-Riemannian geometry, the geometry of spacetime, with care and completeness, and then applies it to special relativity, the Schwarzschild geometry, Robertson–Walker cosmology and causality, ending with the singularity theorems. It is mathematically rigorous and treats Lorentzian geometry on its own terms rather than as a variant of Riemannian geometry. It approaches general relativity firmly from the mathematics first.

Review to follow.

General Relativity, Robert M. Wald. 1st ed., University of Chicago Press, 1984.

Wald is the standard graduate text on general relativity for readers who want the mathematics done properly. Part I covers manifolds, curvature, Einstein’s equation, the Schwarzschild solution and cosmology, using the abstract index notation that has since become standard. Part II covers causal structure, singularity theorems, black holes and quantum effects in curved spacetime. The appendices fill in the mathematics. Its treatment of linearised gravity is thin, so supplement it there.

Physics 3 (Quantum Systems)

Lectures on Quantum Theory, Frederic P. Schuller (lectures); Simon Rea (notes). Lecture course, FAU Erlangen–Nürnberg, 2015.

Schuller’s 2015 lectures at Erlangen take a radical approach. They start from the axioms of quantum mechanics and then develop, in full, the mathematics needed to make sense of them. That means Banach and Hilbert spaces, measure theory and the Lebesgue integral, self-adjoint operators and their domains, and the spectral theorem, before returning to physics with spin, composite systems, the harmonic oscillator and periodic potentials. We finally see why physicists’ casual manipulations of unbounded operators can go wrong. The written notes are by Simon Rea, based on the recorded lectures.

Quantum Mechanics, David Gross. Lecture notes, Universität zu Köln.

David Gross’s notes for the first course in quantum mechanics at the University of Cologne cover wave mechanics, the Hilbert space framework, two-body problems, symmetries, angular momentum, central potentials and perturbation theory, with appendices on complex analysis and distributions. They are written by a mathematical physicist for physics students, which makes them an excellent middle ground between the standard physics texts and the rigorous books on this shelf.

Review to follow. · Free online

Quantum Theory for Mathematicians, Brian C. Hall. 1st ed., Springer (GTM 267), 2013.

Hall wrote quantum mechanics for mathematicians, and it is the book to read if we want to understand what physicists are doing without accepting any hand-waving. It goes from classical mechanics through the spectral theorem and unbounded operators to the Stone–von Neumann theorem, Lie groups and angular momentum, the hydrogen atom, the WKB approximation, path integrals and geometric quantisation. Each topic is treated rigorously, but always with the physics in view.

Review to follow.

Quantum Theory, Groups and Representations: An Introduction, Peter Woit. 1st ed., Springer, 2017.

Woit organises quantum mechanics around symmetry. Lie groups and their representations take us from U(1) and SU(2) through the Heisenberg group and the harmonic oscillator, Clifford algebras and spinors, and the Poincaré group, all the way to free quantum fields and Yang–Mills theory. It assumes some quantum mechanics and shows better than most how much of physics is representation theory in disguise.

Review to follow.

Advanced Quantum Mechanics, David Gross. Lecture notes, Universität zu Köln.

The sequel to Gross’s first course begins with density operators, entanglement and Bell inequalities, reflecting his background in quantum information. It then covers identical particles and second quantisation, the quantised electromagnetic field, symmetries, the Klein–Gordon and Dirac equations and scattering theory. It is a modern advanced course in a form rarely found in textbooks.

Review to follow. · Free online

Mathematical Concepts of Quantum Mechanics, Stephen J. Gustafson, Israel Michael Sigal. 3rd ed., Springer (Universitext), 2020.

Gustafson and Sigal are active researchers in mathematical physics, and their book is a concise graduate introduction to the mathematics of quantum mechanics as it is actually practised. It covers Schrödinger operators, spectral theory, bound states, scattering theory and many-body systems, and later chapters reach quantum fields, path integrals and open quantum systems. It moves faster than Hall and assumes some functional analysis.

Review to follow.

Spectral Theory and Quantum Mechanics, Valter Moretti. 2nd ed., Springer (Unitext 110), 2017.

Moretti’s book is the most complete treatment of the mathematical foundations of quantum mechanics available. It develops Hilbert spaces and the spectral theory of bounded and unbounded operators in full, then uses them to treat the axioms of quantum mechanics, Gleason’s theorem, symmetries through Wigner’s and Kadison’s theorems, superselection rules and the algebraic formulation. At around 950 pages it is enormous, and each edition is larger than the last. Use it as the reference that answers every ‘but is that actually true?’ question.

Review to follow.

Fundamental Mathematical Structures of Quantum Theory, Valter Moretti. 1st ed., Springer, 2019.

This is a more compact companion to Moretti’s larger book, focusing on the spectral theory of unbounded operators, the foundational structure of quantum theory, symmetries and the C*- and von Neumann algebraic formulation. Its MAA reviewer praised it as a resource for spectral theory but noted it is not entirely standalone and works best alongside the larger book. A second edition has been announced.

Physics 4 (Quantum Fields)

Quantum Field Theory, Peter Woit. Lecture notes, Columbia University.

Woit’s lecture notes on quantum field theory are written for mathematicians and continue in the spirit of his book on quantum theory and representations, with symmetry and representation theory leading the way.

Martin Zirnbauer’s lecture notes for the first half of the quantum field theory sequence at the University of Cologne.

What Is a Quantum Field Theory?, Michel Talagrand. 1st ed., Cambridge University Press, 2022.

Talagrand, who received the Abel Prize in 2024, came to quantum field theory as an outsider and wrote the book he wished he had found. Condensed Matter Field Theory, Alexander Altland, Ben Simons. 3rd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2023.

Altland and Simons is the standard graduate text on field-theoretic methods in condensed matter physics. The first part covers second quantisation, functional integrals, perturbation theory, broken symmetry and the renormalisation group. This is where most of us first see a functional integral actually earn its keep.

It is research-level and demanding, but it explains unusually clearly what a quantum field theory is, as opposed to how to calculate with one.

Physics 5 (Statistical Physics)

Entropy, Order Parameters, and Complexity, James P. Sethna. 2nd ed., Oxford University Press, 2021.

Sethna’s book is statistical mechanics for anyone, physicist or not, and its exercises are among the best in any physics book, drawing on biology, computation, economics and engineering as well as physics. It covers random walks, entropy and information, free energies, quantum statistical mechanics, computational methods, order parameters and topological defects, correlations and response, and phase transitions. The treatment of topological defects through homotopy theory is unusual and wonderful. As its Physics Today reviewer noted, it works better as a course text than for self-study. Statistical Mechanics of Lattice Systems: A Concrete Mathematical Introduction, Sacha Friedli, Yvan Velenik. 1st ed., Cambridge University Press, 2017.

Friedli and Velenik wrote a rigorous introduction to equilibrium statistical mechanics that is also remarkably concrete. Each idea is developed through specific models, namely the Curie–Weiss and Ising models, the Gaussian free field and models with continuous symmetry, before the general theory of Gibbs measures, cluster expansions, Pirogov–Sinai theory and reflection positivity. The book is full of figures and complements that connect the material to current research. It assumes only measure-theoretic probability. The authors keep a free version online.

Review to follow. · Free online

Statistical Physics of Particles, Mehran Kardar. 1st ed., Cambridge University Press, 2007.

Kardar’s two volumes come from his MIT graduate course. The first covers thermodynamics, probability, kinetic theory, classical and quantum statistical mechanics and interacting particles. Their distinctive feature is that the problems come with worked solutions, so much of the teaching happens through them. It is the standard modern graduate text for the conventional, non-rigorous approach.

Review to follow.

Statistical Physics of Fields, Mehran Kardar. 1st ed., Cambridge University Press, 2007.

The second volume covers statistical field theory, building from Landau–Ginzburg theory through scaling and universality to the perturbative and position-space renormalisation group, the Kosterlitz–Thouless transition, and dissipative dynamics. Its Physics Today reviewer called the first eight chapters stunning, and the treatment of the renormalisation group is as clear as any available.

Statistical Mechanics: Rigorous Results, David Ruelle. Reprint, World Scientific / Imperial College Press, 1999.

Ruelle’s book from 1969 founded rigorous statistical mechanics as a field. It proves the existence of the thermodynamic limit, treats the ensembles and correlation functions for classical and quantum systems, studies lattice systems and introduces the algebraic approach using C*-algebras. Much of the later research literature builds on this book, and it remains essential reading.

Review to follow.

Gibbs Measures and Phase Transitions, Hans-Otto Georgii. 2nd ed., De Gruyter, 2011.

Georgii’s book is the standard reference on Gibbs measures and phase transitions, built on the Dobrushin–Lanford–Ruelle formulation. It covers the general theory, Markov chains and Gaussian fields, shift-invariant measures and reflection-positive models, and needs only measure theory and probability. Its bibliographical notes, with hundreds of annotated references, are a guide to the whole field. Read it after Friedli and Velenik.

Review to follow.

Statistical Mechanics of Disordered Systems: A Mathematical Perspective, Anton Bovier. 1st ed., Cambridge University Press, 2006.

Bovier gives a rigorous treatment of disordered systems, from the random-field Ising model and the random energy models to the Hopfield model of neural networks and the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick spin glass with its Parisi solution. This is where statistical mechanics meets the hardest open problems in probability, and Bovier is one of its main contributors.

Review to follow.

Operator Algebras and Quantum Statistical Mechanics 1: C*- and W*-Algebras, Symmetry Groups, Decomposition of States, Ola Bratteli, Derek W. Robinson. 2nd ed., Springer, 1987.

Bratteli and Robinson’s two volumes are the standard reference on the operator-algebraic approach to quantum statistical mechanics. Volume 1 develops the necessary mathematics, namely C*- and von Neumann algebras, groups and semigroups of automorphisms (which describe dynamics), and the decomposition theory of states. It is research-level mathematics, but it is clearly written and complete.

Operator Algebras and Quantum Statistical Mechanics 2, Ola Bratteli, Derek W. Robinson. 2nd ed., Springer, 1997.

Volume 2 applies the theory of Volume 1 to physics. It develops the KMS condition for equilibrium states and its relation to stability, and then treats quantum spin systems and continuous systems in detail, including the canonical commutation and anticommutation relations. It has been the standard reference for decades and goes naturally with Haag.

Quantum Physics: A Functional Integral Point of View, James Glimm, Arthur Jaffe. 2nd ed., Springer, 1987.

Glimm and Jaffe founded constructive quantum field theory, the effort to prove that interacting quantum field theories actually exist. This book presents that programme through the Euclidean functional integral, which turns quantum field theory into statistical mechanics. It develops lattice approximations, correlation inequalities and cluster expansions, and constructs interacting field theories in two dimensions. It is demanding, but it is the bridge between Physics 4 and Physics 5.
